Geelong and the Surf Coast have a wonderful mix of established homes: Victorian and Edwardian weatherboards, interwar bungalows, mid-century brick homes, and coastal houses from every decade. Each has its own character — and it's easy to lose that character with a renovation that ignores it.

## Understand what gives the house its character

Walk through the house and note what makes it feel like itself. It might be:

- Ceiling heights and proportions of rooms.
- Original timber floors.
- Skirting boards, architraves and cornices.
- Window and door styles.
- Fireplaces, built-ins or decorative details.
- The way rooms connect, or the way light moves through the house.

These are the elements most worth protecting, even if almost everything else changes.

## Repair before you replace

Original features that look tired are often in better condition than they appear. Timber floors can frequently be repaired and refinished, and original doors and windows can sometimes be restored. Where repair isn't practical, consider whether a feature can be reused elsewhere in the house.

## Match proportions, not just style

A common mistake is choosing finishes that are "in keeping" but at the wrong scale — skirting that's too low for the ceiling height, or doors that are a different height to the originals. Getting proportions right does more for a renovation than any single decorative detail.

## Pay attention to trims and junctions

Skirting, architraves and the way new work meets old are where renovations most often give themselves away. Matching existing profiles, or choosing a complementary profile that sits comfortably beside them, helps new work feel considered. Careful scribing where new joinery meets old, uneven walls makes a real difference.

## Let new elements be honest — and well made

Not everything new needs to imitate the old. A new kitchen or bathroom can be clearly contemporary and still feel like it belongs, if it relates to the rest of the house through materials, colour and proportion. What matters most is that it's well made.

## Plan for the realities of older houses

Older homes often hide surprises: out-of-square walls, uneven floors, previous renovations of varying quality, or outdated wiring and plumbing. A good renovator will allow for investigation, explain what they find and help you decide how to deal with it. Electrical and plumbing upgrades must be carried out by licensed trades.

## Check heritage requirements

Some properties are covered by heritage overlays in the local planning scheme, which can affect external changes. If you're unsure, check with your council before finalising plans.

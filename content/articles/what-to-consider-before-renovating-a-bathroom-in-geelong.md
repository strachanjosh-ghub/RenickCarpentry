A bathroom is one of the hardest-working rooms in a house and one of the most demanding to renovate. Several trades work in a small space, tolerances are tight, and the quality of work you can't see — behind the tiles and under the floor — matters as much as what you can. Here is what we suggest thinking through before work starts.

## 1. Start with how the room needs to work

Before looking at tiles or tapware, write down how the bathroom is used now and how you'd like it to be used:

- Who uses it, and at what times of day?
- Do you need a bath, or would a larger shower be more useful?
- Is storage a daily frustration?
- Are there accessibility needs now or in the coming years — a level-entry shower, grab rail backing in the walls, a wider doorway?

These answers shape the layout, and the layout drives almost every other decision.

## 2. Decide what can move — and what probably shouldn't

Moving a toilet, shower or vanity can transform a bathroom, but it can also add significant plumbing work, especially on a concrete slab. In many older Geelong homes with timber floors there is more flexibility, but every house is different. It's worth understanding the cost and complexity of moving fixtures early, so you can decide whether the improvement is worth it.

## 3. Treat waterproofing and substrate as the priority

The most expensive bathroom problems are usually hidden ones: water getting where it shouldn't. Ask how the floor and walls will be prepared, how waterproofing will be done and who is responsible for it. A beautiful finish laid over a poor substrate will not last.

## 4. Plan ventilation and lighting properly

Moisture is the enemy of a bathroom's finishes, and coastal homes in places like Torquay, Ocean Grove and Barwon Heads can be especially prone to it. A well-sized exhaust fan ducted to the outside, plus good natural ventilation where possible, protects paint, joinery and grout. Layered lighting — good general light plus clear light at the mirror — makes the room far more pleasant to use. Electrical work must be done by a licensed electrician.

## 5. Choose storage before you choose finishes

Built-in storage, recessed niches and a well-designed vanity make a bathroom feel calm and uncluttered. These need to be planned before framing and plumbing are finalised, because a niche or a wall-hung vanity depends on what's inside the wall.

## 6. Understand the trades and approvals involved

A typical bathroom renovation can involve demolition, carpentry, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing, tiling, plastering and painting. Plumbing and electrical work must be carried out by appropriately licensed practitioners. Depending on the scope and value of the work, other requirements may also apply, so ask who is responsible for what and whether any permits are needed. Your local council or a registered building surveyor can confirm requirements for your property.

## 7. Then choose finishes — with durability in mind

Once the layout and services are settled, finishes are much easier to choose. Think about:

- **Cleaning:** large-format tiles mean fewer grout lines; some textured surfaces trap more grime.
- **Slip resistance:** especially for shower floors and for anyone with mobility concerns.
- **Consistency with the house:** in an older home, a bathroom that nods to the original character often feels more "right" than a generic modern fit-out.

## 8. Be realistic about timing

Product lead times and trade scheduling affect how long a bathroom takes. A good renovator will give you realistic expectations once the design and selections are settled rather than a one-size-fits-all number. If this is your only bathroom, plan for how you'll manage while it's out of action.
